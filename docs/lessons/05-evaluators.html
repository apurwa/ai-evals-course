Lesson 5/Module 2/~65 min

Automated Evaluators

Build an evaluator, then prove it agrees with you before you let it tell you anything.

01 Objective

You can build a judge for a failure mode you found in L4, measure whether it agrees with human labels, and correct the success rate it reports for the fact that it is imperfect.

02 The failure this prevents

Trusting an unvalidated judge. Someone writes a prompt ending in "respond PASS or FAIL", runs it over a thousand traces, and reports 91 percent. Nobody ever checked whether the judge and a human agree. It might be 91 percent. It might be measuring response length.

The specific trap is the one the lab opens with. A judge for a rare failure can report 97 percent agreement with humans and still catch none of it, because agreeing with "no failure here" 97 percent of the time is exactly what saying nothing achieves. Overall agreement is close to meaningless under class imbalance, and it is the number people quote.

03 Concepts
Code check versus judge
If the property is decidable in code, decide it in code. "Did it call check_return_eligibility" is a set membership test, not a language problem. Reach for a model only for things that genuinely need reading: tone, whether an explanation matches the actual reason, whether a refusal was helpful.
True positive rate (recall, sensitivity)
TPR = TP / (TP + FN). Of the failures that were really there, what fraction did the judge catch? A judge that never fires has TPR 0.
True negative rate (specificity)
TNR = TN / (TN + FP). Of the runs that were fine, what fraction did the judge leave alone? A judge that always fires has TNR 0.
Why both, always
Either one alone is trivially gamed. Report them as a pair, aim for above 0.8 each, and prefer above 0.9 before you let a judge gate a release.
Cohen's kappa
Agreement corrected for the agreement you would get by chance. Above 0.8 is excellent, 0.6 to 0.8 is workable, below 0.6 means your criteria are ambiguous and the fix is to rewrite them rather than to relabel.
Bias correction
An imperfect judge reports a biased success rate. If you know its TPR and TNR you can correct for that and put a confidence interval on the result. The judgy library does this and it is a two-line change.

Accuracy is the wrong headline number

Recalled items are about two percent of this corpus. A judge that always says "fine" scores 98 percent accuracy on that failure and catches none of it. Nothing about that number is a lie, and everything about reporting it is misleading. Report TPR and TNR.

04 Mental model
                        actually broken    actually fine
    judge says broken         TP                FP        <- false alarms
    judge says fine           FN                TN
                              ^
                              misses

    TPR = TP/(TP+FN)   caught / really broken
    TNR = TN/(TN+FP)   left alone / really fine

    accuracy = (TP+TN)/all   <- hides both when classes are imbalanced

Build the judge, fill in this table against human labels, and only then look at what it says about your agent. In that order, every time.

05 Browser lab
Lab 5/the other centerpiece

Judge playground

Opens with the imbalance trap, live, on real corpus counts. Then you build a judge from composable checks and watch the confusion matrix, TPR, TNR and kappa move as you add and remove them. Every check is a real predicate evaluated over real traces, so a judge that looks good here would look the same in CI.

Take the best judge you built in the browser, implement it in evals/, and score it with sklearn.metrics.cohen_kappa_score against the corpus labels. Then feed its TPR and TNR to judgy and compare the corrected success rate with the raw one. The gap between those two numbers is the amount you would have been wrong by.

07 Traps
  • Check that you reported TPR and TNR, not accuracy. If someone asks for one number, give them the pair anyway and explain why in a sentence.
  • Check that your judge was validated on data it did not help create. Tuning a judge on the same traces you measure it with tells you nothing.
  • Check whether a code check would do. A model asked to count tool calls is a slow, expensive, occasionally wrong in operator.
  • Check kappa, not raw agreement. Two annotators who both say "fine" 95 percent of the time agree 90 percent of the time by accident.
  • Check that a low kappa sends you back to the criteria. The instinct is to relabel until it goes up. The cause is almost always an ambiguous definition.
  • Check that the judge cannot see the answer. A judge shown the expected outcome will score well and mean nothing.
08 Self-check
  • A judge reports 96 percent agreement on a failure with 3 percent prevalence. What is the most likely value of its TPR?
  • Your kappa is 0.45. What do you change first, and what do you not change?
  • When is a language model the wrong tool for an evaluator?
  • Your judge has TPR 0.85 and TNR 0.92 and reports a 70 percent success rate. Is the true rate higher or lower, and what do you need to say about it?
