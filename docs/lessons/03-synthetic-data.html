Lesson 3/Module 1/~50 min

Synthetic Data and Scenarios

Generate a workload you control end to end, so that ground truth exists by construction instead of by labelling.

01 Objective

You can build a test set whose correct answers are computed rather than guessed, and you can say which parts of the input space it covers and which it does not.

02 The failure this prevents

Someone writes forty test cases by hand in an afternoon. They feel representative. They are not: they are forty variations on the cases that came to mind, which are the cases that are easy to think of, which are the cases the agent already handles.

The specific damage is silent. Your suite passes at 95 percent and the missing five percent is not a hard slice of the problem, it is an absent slice. Nobody wrote a test for a recalled item because nobody thought about recalls. The agent has never been asked. You will find out from a customer.

03 Concepts
Dimension-based generation
Enumerate the axes that change the correct answer (tier, condition, order status, final sale, hazmat, recall), then generate the tuples rather than the sentences. The wording is decoration; the tuple is the test.
Grounding in real rows
Every scenario points at a real order line in the database. This is what lets rules.py compute the expected answer. A scenario invented in prose has no computable correct answer and quietly becomes an opinion.
Coverage sampling
One scenario per distinct dimension profile. Answers "is there any case at all for this combination". Used by L6.
Prevalence sampling
Sampled in proportion to how often combinations actually occur. Answers "what is the success rate a customer would experience". Used for the confidence intervals in L5.
Boundary sampling
Deliberately oversampled at limits: day 29, day 30, day 31; one cent under the approval limit, exactly on it, one cent over. Answers "does the rule fire in the right place".
Theoretical saturation
Borrowed from qualitative research and used properly in L4. Here it is the reason to stop generating: when new scenarios stop producing new dimension profiles, more scenarios buy you sample size, not coverage.

These three are not interchangeable, and mixing them up is the classic error

A coverage-sampled suite tells you nothing about your real success rate, because it deliberately over-represents rare cases. A prevalence-sampled suite will happily contain zero recalled items, because recalls are rare, and will therefore report excellent health while an entire safety path goes untested. You need both, kept separate, and labelled. Every scenario in this repository carries a sampling field for exactly this reason.

04 Mental model
rulebook allows        480 combinations   (6 tiers x conditions x statuses x 3 flags)
world produces         115 combinations   <-- the rest cannot occur
scenarios sample       115 combinations   <-- full coverage of what exists

the 365 difference is not a bug in the suite.
it is the set of questions your test data can never ask.
knowing the number is the deliverable.

Most teams never compute the middle line. They compare the bottom line to their own expectations and conclude they have good coverage, which is true and useless.

Then break it on purpose. In data/world/facts.yaml, set generation.edge_case_targets.recalled_item_orders to 0 and rebuild with make world && make check.

The coverage gate should fail and name item_recalled as unreachable. That is the guard working: a whole safety path just vanished from the test suite and nothing else in the system noticed or cared. Put the value back.

Where a model helps, and where it does not

Generating the tuples is deterministic code, because the tuples are the test and must be exhaustive. A model is genuinely useful for the wording: paraphrasing one scenario into twenty voices, registers, and levels of confusion. Note the split. The model varies what does not matter to correctness, and code owns what does.

07 Traps
  • Check that your expected answers are computed, not written. If a human typed the expected output, you have two systems that can be wrong and no way to tell which is.
  • Check whether your generator can produce the case you are worried about. If it cannot, your suite will never fail on it, no matter how large you make it.
  • Check that you know which sampling strategy each number came from. A success rate from a coverage-sampled suite is not a success rate.
  • Check for a frozen clock. Any scenario whose correct answer depends on today's date will silently change meaning, and your regressions will look like model drift.
  • Check that synthetic phrasing is not uniformly polite. Real customers write in fragments, with typos, mid-complaint, and about two orders at once.
08 Self-check
  • Why does every scenario point at a real database row rather than describing one?
  • Your suite is 100 percent coverage-sampled and reports 88 percent. What can you tell a product manager about the experience customers are having?
  • Recalls are 1.5 percent of orders. Under prevalence sampling, how many recall scenarios would you expect in a 200-case suite, and why is that a problem?
  • Which part of scenario generation should a language model do, and which part must it not?
