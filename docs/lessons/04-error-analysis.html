Error Analysis

Read traces until you know what is actually going wrong, in language specific enough that somebody could write a test for it.

01 Objective

You can turn a pile of traces into a failure taxonomy with named, countable categories, and you can say when you have read enough.

02 The failure this prevents

Jumping straight to metrics. A team that has not read its traces picks the metrics it can imagine, which are almost always fluency, helpfulness, and a vague notion of accuracy. Six weeks later all three look fine and the product is still bad, because the real failure was that the agent never consults the policy tool and nobody was measuring that, because nobody knew.

This is the single highest-leverage lesson in the course, and it is the one people skip, because reading a hundred traces by hand feels like something a machine should do. The machine can help you later. It cannot tell you what to look for, because that is exactly the thing you do not yet know.

03 Concepts
Open coding
Read one trace, write a short phrase describing what went wrong, in your own words, without a predefined list. Repeat. The vocabulary emerges from the data instead of being imposed on it.
Axial coding
Once you have forty phrases, group them. "Didn't check eligibility", "used remembered policy", and "quoted a fee from nowhere" collapse into failed to consult source of truth. The groups are your taxonomy.
Theoretical saturation
The point where new traces stop producing new categories. In practice: read 50 and you might find 10 categories, the next 25 add 2, the next 25 add none. Stop. Anything further buys sample size, not understanding.
Prevalence versus severity
The most common failure is rarely the most important one. Count both, and keep them in separate columns. A weekly under-refund matters more than a daily clumsy sentence.
A good failure name
Specific enough that two people would label the same trace the same way, and that somebody could write a check for it. "Bad answer" fails both tests. "Refunded without calling check_return_eligibility" passes both.

Do not start from a taxonomy, including this repository's

The lab hides its failure labels until after you have coded. This is deliberate. If you read the categories first you will find exactly those categories and nothing else, which feels productive and teaches you nothing. The value of open coding is entirely in the codes that surprise you.

05 Browser lab
Lab 4/the centerpiece

Annotation tool with live saturation curve

120 traces with their labels hidden. Read each one, write what went wrong in your own words, and watch your saturation curve build in real time. When it flattens, stop, do the axial pass to group your codes, and only then reveal the planted taxonomy to see what you caught, what you missed, and what you found that it does not contain.

Your work is saved in the browser, so you can do this over several sittings. It is meant to take a while. That is the lesson.

07 Traps
  • Check that your categories are specific enough to write a test for. If you cannot imagine the assertion, the category is a feeling.
  • Check that you stopped because the curve flattened, not because you got bored or the meeting was on Thursday.
  • Check that two people would label the same trace the same way. If not, the problem is your category names, and L5 will fail for the same reason.
  • Check that you counted severity separately from frequency. Sorting by count alone will send you to fix the most common cosmetic issue.
  • Check that you read whole traces, not just final responses. Half the failures in this corpus are invisible from the last message.
  • Check that you are not coding the same trace twice under two names. One trace, one primary failure. Note the secondary separately if you must.
08 Self-check
  • How do you know when to stop reading traces, and what does the answer not depend on?
  • Give a failure name that would fail an inter-annotator agreement check, and fix it.
  • Your most frequent category is "response slightly too long". What should you do?
  • Why does the lab hide its labels until after you code?
  • What can a language model do for you in this process, and at what point?
