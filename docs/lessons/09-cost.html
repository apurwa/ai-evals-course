Lesson 9/Module 5/~55 min

Cost, Latency and the Improve Loop

Find the frontier where accuracy meets cost, choose a point on it deliberately, and change one thing at a time against a test set you have not been reading.

01 Objective

You can locate where your spend actually goes, decide which point on the accuracy and cost frontier your product needs, and run an improvement loop that does not fool you.

02 The failure this prevents

Optimising by vibes. Somebody says the agent feels expensive, so the team switches to a cheaper model, quality drops in a way nobody measures precisely, and three weeks later they switch back. Nothing was learned because nothing was isolated.

The related failure is reward hacking your own loop. You iterate against the test set, quality climbs beautifully, and you have optimised for that specific set of scenarios. The number goes up. The product does not improve. This is why the test split is frozen and read once.

03 Concepts
Cost attribution
Per-span cost, so the question "what should we cut" has an answer. A session total supports exactly one decision, which is to use the whole agent less.
The Pareto frontier
The set of configurations where you cannot improve accuracy without paying more, or reduce cost without losing accuracy. Everything not on the frontier is strictly worse than something else and should be discarded without discussion.
Choosing a point
The frontier is a fact about your system. Which point to sit on is a product decision: what a failure costs you, what a customer will tolerate, what margin the feature needs. Engineering finds the curve, the product owner picks the spot.
Step removal beats step improvement
Success compounds, so deleting a step often buys more than making one better, and it is cheaper and faster too. Look for steps whose output nothing consumes.
Prompt caching
A long static system prompt re-sent every turn is usually the largest single line in an agent's bill and the easiest to fix.
The improve loop
Change one thing, run the dev split, keep it if it helps, write down why. Touch the test split once, at the end. If you cannot say which change produced a gain, you have not learned anything you can repeat.
Reward hacking
Optimising the measurement rather than the thing. If your judge rewards long explanations, you will get long explanations, and your metric will look excellent.

The numbers you can and cannot trust here

Cost arithmetic and the shape of the distribution are real, computed from the corpus. The absolute dollars are not a measurement: token counts are placeholders because no model was called, and the prices in evals/pricing.json are marked verified: false. The frontier configurations in the lab are explicitly illustrative and are labelled as such on the page. With an API key, make corpus replaces every one of those with a number you measured.

07 Traps
  • Check that you changed one thing. Two changes and a result is a story, not a finding.
  • Check that you have not been iterating against the test set. If you have, its number is a dev number now and you need a fresh split.
  • Check whether a step can be deleted before trying to improve it. Compounding usually favours deletion.
  • Check that your judge cannot be gamed by the change you are making. If longer answers score better, a change that lengthens answers will look like a win.
  • Check that your prices are current. Model pricing moves, and a frontier drawn on last year's prices recommends last year's answer.
  • Check latency separately from cost. They usually correlate and occasionally invert, and users feel one of them.
08 Self-check
  • Your first model call is 60 percent of spend. What is the fix, and why is it not "use a smaller model"?
  • Two configs: 0.88 accuracy at $9, and 0.86 at $14. What do you do with the second?
  • Who decides which point on the frontier to ship, and what do they need to know?
  • Your dev score rose 6 points and test rose 1. What most likely happened?
